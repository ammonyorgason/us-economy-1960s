The research question

A strong decade with a warning sign

Using real GDP, unemployment, and inflation, this case study evaluates the U.S. economy from 1960 to 1969. Output rose and joblessness fell—but inflation accelerated late in the expansion.

02 · Historical context

Three turning points shaped the decade

1960–1961

Recession and rebound

The NBER dates the recession to April 1960–February 1961. Quarterly output declined in parts of 1960; by 1961, output was recovering even as annual unemployment averaged 6.7% (with a 7.1% monthly peak in May).

1964–1966

Expansion accelerates

Real GDP growth reached 6.6% in 1966 as unemployment fell to 3.8%. The 1964 tax cut and Vietnam-era defense spending are plausible contributors, although this study does not quantify their effects.

1967–1969

Inflation takes hold

In 1969, GDP growth was 3.1%, unemployment 3.5%, and CPI inflation 5.5%. Fiscal pressure, monetary policy, and inflation expectations offer possible explanations; the chart alone cannot isolate their effects.

Methodology

Methodology, sources, and credit

Project credit. Interactive portfolio adaptation of Group 7’s 12-slide academic presentation, The U.S. Economy in the 1960s. The original research was collaborative.

Data sources and measurement. Annual real GDP levels have been independently checked against BEA/FRED GDPCA and 1960–61 quarterly annualized growth against BEA/FRED A191RL1Q225SBEA (November 19, 2026). The portfolio uses official annual GDPCA values and the original presentation’s annual growth, inflation, and unemployment figures. Unemployment is documented specifically as the BLS annual-average unemployment rate series; other historical federal compilations may show slightly different figures because of series definitions or revisions. Annual GDP levels are chained 2017-dollar quantities, whereas quarterly rates are seasonally adjusted annualized changes.

Interpretation limits. LRAS positioned at 4.0% growth, curve slopes, the 1.8% benchmark inflation, and the precise path of the illustrative shifts are assumptions imported from the original classroom model. Diagram movements illustrate one possible interpretation of shocks rather than demonstrating that the shocks occurred exactly as pictured. In particular, the recession story, the mid-1960s boom, and the late-1960s inflation episode should be read as historically informed explanations, not as causal effects identified by the three plotted series alone.
